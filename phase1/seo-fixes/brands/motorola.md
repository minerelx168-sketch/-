# Motorola IMEI Check — Free Lookup for Motorola Devices

Motorola is one of the oldest brands in mobile — it invented the modern cell phone in 1973 — and today, under Lenovo ownership, it's the fourth-largest smartphone maker in the Americas and a top three brand in Brazil, Mexico, and Chile. Motorola's portfolio spans three distinct lines: the flagship-tier Edge series, the enormously popular Moto G budget-to-midrange family that dominates US prepaid shelves, and the returning Razr foldables that reinvented the iconic clamshell. Every Motorola device ships with a unique 15-digit IMEI (dual IMEIs on all 4G and 5G units), and understanding what that IMEI reveals is especially useful for the used and prepaid resale market Motorola serves.

Motorola devices are heavily concentrated in the US prepaid segment through Boost Mobile, Cricket, MetroPCS, Straight Talk, and TracFone. That means a very high percentage of used Motos on the market are carrier-locked, and buyers frequently import Latin American Motorolas that use different 5G bands than the US variants. An IMEI check tells you which SKU you're actually buying and whether it's ready to activate on your network.

Our free Motorola IMEI lookup identifies your model instantly. Premium checks reveal blocklist status, carrier lock, and warranty coverage.

## How to Find Your Motorola IMEI

Four straightforward methods:

- **Dial `*#06#`** on the Phone app — the IMEI (both slots if dual SIM) appears immediately.
- **Settings → About phone → IMEI** — works on any Motorola running Android 11 or later.
- **Original retail box** — Motorola prints the IMEI, MEID, and model number on the outer barcode label.
- **SIM tray or rear label** — Moto G and Moto E budget models often engrave the IMEI on the SIM tray; older Razrs used a rear sticker beneath the battery cover.

For Motorola Edge devices with eSIM support, both IMEIs are listed under **Settings → About phone → SIM status**.

## When Should You Run a Motorola IMEI Check?

- **Buying a US prepaid Moto second-hand.** Boost, Cricket, and MetroPCS aggressively lock their devices for 6-12 months after activation. A cheap Moto G on Facebook Marketplace is worthless to you if it's still locked to a carrier you don't use.
- **Importing from Latin America.** LATAM Motos are usually unlocked but support different LTE and 5G bands. An Edge 40 Pro from Brazil may not get 5G on T-Mobile US. The full device report confirms the exact regional SKU.
- **Blacklist verification.** Motorola's US prepaid dominance means the brand appears frequently on GSMA blocklists after theft or lost-device reports. Always blacklist-check before paying cash.
- **Warranty verification.** Motorola runs manufacturer warranty separately from carrier warranty — a device sold by Boost Mobile may have both. Our warranty check confirms remaining manufacturer coverage.
- **Counterfeit or tampered devices.** Fake Moto Gs turn up in Southeast Asian markets, and repair shops occasionally swap logic boards on flagship Razrs. A mismatched IMEI is the fastest tell.
- **ThinkPhone enterprise use.** ThinkPhone units are often deployed under Lenovo enterprise fleet management. If you're buying one used, verify no MDM-style enterprise lock remains.

## What to Do If Your Motorola IMEI Check Reveals a Problem

If the carrier lock check shows a US prepaid lock (Boost, Cricket, MetroPCS, Straight Talk, Verizon prepaid), do not pay full unlocked-device price. Locked prepaid devices are worth significantly less on the secondary market until the lock clears — typically 6 to 12 months from activation, and only if all monthly payments were made on time.

If the blacklist status returns positive, treat the transaction as void. Motorola devices sold through US carriers are frequently financed, and if the original buyer stops payment, the carrier will blacklist the IMEI even though you paid full price in cash — leaving you with a paperweight.

If the model does not match the seller's description (a Moto G Power 5G sold as an Edge 50 Ultra, for example), reject the device — an IMEI mismatch usually means a swapped logic board or an outright counterfeit, both of which void any remaining warranty and make the device unsupportable at authorised repair centres.
